Website maintenance should cover eight jobs: software updates, tested backups, security monitoring, uptime checks, speed checks, form and booking tests, search health checks and small content changes. A good care plan says how often each one happens, who does it, and what you get told afterwards. If a plan only says "updates and hosting", ask what is missing.

Many small business websites are not hacked because someone chose the business. They are hacked because an automated scan found an out-of-date plugin. Maintenance is the routine that stops that from happening, and it also catches the quieter faults: a broken enquiry form, an expired certificate, a page that has slowed to a crawl.

## Why does a website need maintenance after launch?

A website is software, and software ages. Patchstack's [State of WordPress Security in 2026](https://patchstack.com/whitepaper/state-of-wordpress-security-in-2026/) counted 11,334 new vulnerabilities in the WordPress ecosystem in 2025, up 42% on 2024. It found 91% were in plugins, and 46% had no fix from the developer by the time they were made public. Its median time to mass exploitation for heavily exploited vulnerabilities was five hours.

The cost of getting it wrong is real. The Australian Signals Directorate's [Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/annual-cyber-threat-report-2025-26/year-in-review) puts the average self-reported cost of cybercrime for a small business at A$55,400 per report.

## The eight-point website maintenance checklist

| Job | What it means | How often |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Software updates | Core platform, theme and plugin updates, tested before they go live | Weekly, and within 48 hours for a known exploited flaw |
| 2. Tested backups | Full copies of files and database, stored away from the hosting server, with a test restore | Daily backup, restore test every quarter |
| 3. Security monitoring | Malware scans, login protection, firewall rules and alerts on suspicious changes | Continuous, with a monthly summary |
| 4. Uptime monitoring | An outside service checks the site is online and alerts the team when it is not | Every few minutes |
| 5. Certificates and domain | SSL certificate renewal and domain renewal dates tracked | Checked monthly |
| 6. Speed checks | Page speed and Core Web Vitals reviewed, images and scripts kept lean | Monthly |
| 7. Forms, bookings and integrations | Test enquiries sent through every form, booking calendar and CRM connection | Monthly, and after every update |
| 8. Search health | Broken links, indexing errors and Search Console warnings fixed | Monthly |

The update timing follows Australian guidance. The Essential Eight, published by the [Australian Signals Directorate](https://www.cyber.gov.au/resources-business-and-government/essential-cyber-security/essential-eight/essential-eight-maturity-model-changes), sets patching of internet-facing services within two weeks of release, or within 48 hours when a working exploit exists. A public website is an internet-facing service.

## Which jobs do business owners most often miss?

Two jobs on the list cost service businesses the most when they fail, and both are invisible until someone checks.

**Forms and bookings.** An update can quietly break an enquiry form or the connection to your CRM. The site looks fine, but enquiries stop arriving. For an accounting practice or a mortgage broker, a week of lost enquiries can cost more than the maintenance itself. Every care plan should send a test enquiry through each form and confirm it lands in the CRM.

**Restore tests.** A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup. Ask your provider when they last restored your site from a backup, and how long it took.

## What should a website care plan tell you each month?

A short monthly report is the proof that the work is being done. It should list:

- updates applied, and any held back with the reason
- the date of the last backup and the last restore test
- security events blocked or investigated
- uptime for the month
- speed results for the key pages
- forms tested, with the result
- search errors found and fixed

If the report never changes, or never arrives, the work may not be happening.

## Questions to ask before you sign a website care plan

1. **Who owns the hosting account and the domain?** They should be in your business's name. See [who owns your website, domain and data](/blog/who-owns-your-website-domain-and-data).
2. **Are updates tested before they go live?** A staging copy of the site catches problems before your customers do.
3. **Where are backups kept, and for how long?** Copies should sit away from the hosting server and go back at least 30 days.
4. **What happens if the site is hacked?** Ask whether clean-up is included or charged by the hour.
5. **How many content changes are included?** Small edits such as new staff photos or updated prices are often where costs creep in.
6. **What are the support hours?** Check that the hours written in the plan match what you need.

## Can I maintain my own website?

You can, if you have the time and a test routine. The risk is not the update itself; it is skipping the checks afterwards. If you do it yourself, keep a checklist, take a backup before every update, and send a test enquiry through every form once the update is done.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is website hosting the same as website maintenance?

No. Hosting is the server your website runs on. Maintenance is the work that keeps the website on that server updated, backed up, secure and working. Some plans bundle both, so check what is included.

### How often should a small business website be updated?

Check for updates weekly. Apply security fixes for a known exploited flaw within 48 hours, in line with the Essential Eight, and other updates within two weeks.

### Does a website built on a hosted platform still need maintenance?

Less of it. Hosted platforms update their own software, but you still need backups of your content, form and booking tests, speed checks and search health checks.
