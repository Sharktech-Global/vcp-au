Most service businesses now run some automation without thinking of it as "AI".

A form that sorts enquiries. A lead score in the CRM. An AI receptionist that decides which callers get a booking and which get a call back.

From **10 December 2026**, that matters for your privacy policy.

## What changes on 10 December 2026

The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 added new transparency rules to Australian Privacy Principle 1. They apply to organisations already covered by the Privacy Act.

Your privacy policy must say so when all three of these are true:

1. A **computer program** makes a decision, or does something substantially and directly related to making one.
2. The decision could reasonably be expected to **significantly affect** a person's rights or interests.
3. The person's **personal information** is used to run the program.

Where that applies, the policy must describe:

- the **kinds of personal information** the program uses
- the **kinds of decisions made by the program alone**
- the **kinds of decisions where the program does something substantially related**, even if a person makes the final call

"Computer program" is read broadly. Law firm commentary on the change notes it can cover machine learning, rule-based workflows and even a spreadsheet formula. The privacy regulator's message is simple: if in doubt, disclose.

## Where service businesses get caught

The rule is not only for banks and insurers. Look at what your own systems do.

| Automation | Could it be caught? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| AI receptionist that books appointments | Usually low risk | It schedules. It does not decide eligibility. |
| AI receptionist that screens callers out ("we cannot help you") | Possibly | Refusing service can count as a decision. |
| Lead scoring that decides who gets a call back | Possibly | It changes who is served, using personal information. |
| Automated pre-qualification for a loan or service | Likely | It affects access to a service or product. |
| Reminder emails and SMS | Unlikely on its own | It informs. It does not decide. |

The pattern: **informing and booking is lower risk. Filtering, ranking and refusing is where disclosure is needed.**

## A short checklist before 10 December

1. **List every automation that touches client data.** Include your CRM workflows, chatbot, AI receptionist and any online forms with rules.
2. **Mark the ones that decide or filter.** Who gets a booking, a call back, a quote or a "no".
3. **Write down the personal information each one uses.** Name, phone, income, postcode, health or financial details.
4. **Keep a person in the loop for anything significant.** An AI receptionist should capture details and book, then hand eligibility and advice to your team.
5. **Update your privacy policy.** Add a plain-English section on automated decisions, linked from your website footer.
6. **Call out sensitive information clearly.** Clinics and allied health practices handling health information should name it.
7. **Review it every time you add a new workflow.** The policy has to match what your systems actually do.

## Accountants, brokers and clinics: what to watch

**Accounting and bookkeeping practices.** Most automation here is reminders and document requests. Low risk. But if a workflow decides which clients are offered a service or a payment plan, describe it.

**Mortgage and finance brokers.** Brokers already work under the best interests duty in the National Consumer Credit Protection Act. Any automated pre-qualification or lead ranking should be disclosed, and credit assistance should stay with the licensed or authorised broker, never the bot.

**Clinics and allied health.** Health information is sensitive information. If an AI receptionist triages callers or decides urgency, say so, and keep clinical judgement with clinicians.
